Research

Questions at the intersection
of data and society.

My research examines how privacy concerns, trust, and technological change influence individual decisions, innovation incentives, and economic growth. I combine theoretical modelling with empirical methods, including survey data analysis, Bayesian model averaging, and generic machine learning inference.

Job market paper
Innovation · Growth · Privacy

Data-Driven Innovation and Privacy Concerns in a Schumpeterian Growth Model

With Haiping Zhang and Alexandre Dmitriev

This paper develops a Schumpeterian growth model in which consumer data are used as the R&D input for quality improvements, and consumers balance the economic benefits of selling their consumption data against privacy concerns. In the market equilibrium, a higher degree of privacy concerns reduces the supply of consumer data, hindering innovation and thereby slowing down growth. This finding is consistent with our cross-country empirical evidence using Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA), which accounts for model uncertainty and shows that privacy concerns are robustly negatively correlated with regional digital development. Furthermore, our model shows that if the degree of privacy concerns exceeds a certain threshold, the decentralized equilibrium features socially excessive innovation, suggesting that data use should be more strictly regulated or taxed. In contrast, if the degree of privacy concerns is lower than this threshold value, innovation in the decentralized equilibrium is below the socially efficient level, and policies that promote or subsidize data use become socially desirable. These findings highlight the role of privacy concerns in shaping data availability and innovation outcomes and help us understand cross-country differences in data regulation policies.

Behavioural economics · Survey data

Privacy Concerns, Trust, and Monitoring: Evidence from Europe

This project studies the trade-off between privacy and government monitoring using the European Social Survey. It examines how a pandemic-related framing shifts stated preferences toward monitoring, and whether responses differ across individuals. The analysis combines survey-based comparisons with machine-learning methods for heterogeneous effects, highlighting institutional trust as an important dimension of variation in privacy–monitoring attitudes. An extension of this analysis employs machine-learning methods to predict the unobserved privacy preferences of non-respondents and assess the potential implications of non-response bias for the survey findings.
